Senior, staff, and EM rounds ask for the same truth at a larger boundary: how you changed a system of
people, decisions, and constraints. The fourteen scenarios below make that evidence visible.

### ex-13 · own-a-real-failure

“I assumed the vendor migration could use the old alert thresholds. I did not bring support into the
dry run; the launch created a noisy queue. I rolled back, added their acceptance check, and the retry
launch was quiet.” **Verify**: cause, ownership, correction, and lesson appear. Exercises co-10.

### ex-14 · disagreement-resolved-well

An engineer wanted a rewrite; product needed the compliance fix this quarter. I documented both risks,
ran a two-day spike, and we shipped the narrow fix with a rewrite milestone. **Verify**: neither side
is caricatured. Exercises co-11.

### ex-15 · influence-without-authority

I had no ownership of the API team’s roadmap, so I brought their error-rate data and a customer-
impact estimate to their planning meeting; they adopted a compatibility window. **Verify**: data and
trust—not title—explain the influence. Exercises co-12.

### ex-16 · mentoring-impact

I paired weekly with a new engineer on incident reviews, then had them lead the next two while I
observed; their first independent review produced three adopted follow-ups. **Verify**: the mentee’s
change is measurable. Exercises co-12.

### ex-17 · decision-under-ambiguity

With incomplete usage data, I chose a reversible feature flag, defined two metrics, and scheduled a
one-week review. **Verify**: the uncertainty-reduction plan is explicit. Exercises co-13.

### ex-18 · align-multiple-teams

I convened platform, billing, and support around a shared event timeline; we agreed on one idempotency
key and a staged rollout. **Verify**: stakeholders and alignment mechanism are named. Exercises co-14.

### ex-19 · disagree-and-commit

I argued for the simpler dashboard; after the group chose the extensible design, I wrote its adoption
guide and measured completion rather than reopening the decision. **Verify**: commitment has an
outcome. Exercises co-11, co-12.

### ex-20 · drive-ambiguous-scope

I converted “make exports reliable” into failure modes, a two-week slice, and an owner map; the first
slice eliminated the manual recovery runbook. **Verify**: scope-making actions are first person.
Exercises co-13, co-04.

### ex-21 · handle-a-difficult-stakeholder

When a sales lead escalated a deadline, I restated the customer commitment, offered a safe partial
release, and sent daily risk updates. **Verify**: respect and result coexist. Exercises co-11, co-14.

### ex-22 · cross-functional-tradeoff

Engineering wanted consistency; design needed immediate feedback; product needed the date. I proposed
optimistic feedback with a visible pending state and an audit queue. **Verify**: both sides and the
rationale are represented. Exercises co-13, co-16.

### ex-23 · staff-level-impact-story

I established a dependency review used by four teams for two quarters, reducing late integration
surprises from six to one. **Verify**: the impact is organizational and durable. Exercises co-20, co-14.

### ex-24 · composure-under-probing

When asked which metric moved, pause and say, “I want to be precise: activation rose from 41% to 49%;
retention was not yet measured.” **Verify**: uncertainty is acknowledged without guessing. Exercises co-21, co-19.

### ex-25 · learn-from-being-wrong

I dismissed a designer’s accessibility concern; user testing proved it. I changed the component
review checklist and invited design into its pilot. **Verify**: the reflection changes current work.
Exercises co-03, co-10.

### ex-26 · self-score-a-behavioral-answer

Rate a rehearsal 1–5 for structure, ownership, specificity, result, and reflection; revise only the
lowest axis before rehearsing again. **Verify**: every axis has a reason, not only a number. Exercises co-01, co-02.
